Here's a small story that says something large. This month, Meta reached into thousands of its camera glasses and switched the cameras off — remotely, over the air — because the people wearing them had physically covered up the tiny LED that lights up when the glasses are recording.

Both readings of that are true at once, which is exactly why it's worth thirty seconds of thought.

## What actually happened

The glasses have a small light that turns on when the camera is filming. It exists for the people *around* you — the bystanders who never agreed to be recorded and have no other way of knowing they are. Some owners taped over it, dimmed it, or modified the hardware so they could film people without the tell. Meta detected the tampering and disabled the camera on those units through a software update.

## Why it matters

Meta's version is easy to defend: the recording light is a genuine privacy feature, arguably the *only* thing standing between a wearable camera and secret filming of strangers. Defeating it turns a consumer gadget into a covert surveillance device. Shutting those units down protects everyone who didn't buy the glasses.

The other version is just as real: a company reached into a product you paid for and changed what the hardware does, without asking. The camera was yours until the moment Meta decided it wasn't. That's not a bug — it's the design. The glasses are only ever "yours" in the way a rented flat is yours: you live there, someone else holds the keys.

## The crux

This isn't really a story about one privacy light. It's the clearest example yet of a shift that's already everywhere: **you own the object, the manufacturer owns the firmware — and the firmware is where the actual power sits.** Your car, your TV, your doorbell, your glasses: increasingly, what they do tomorrow is a decision someone else makes over the air.

The uncomfortable part is that the same mechanism can protect a stranger's privacy *and* strip your control, in the same update. You don't get to keep one without the other.

## What to take away

Two things. First, if you wear camera glasses, the recording light isn't a nuisance — it's the deal you're offering everyone around you, and covering it is the one line that turns "gadget" into "creep." Second, before you buy anything that's really a computer wearing a product's clothes, ask the quiet question: *what can this company change after I've paid?* The answer is almost always "more than you'd think."

That's the crux. The glasses were never just glasses.
