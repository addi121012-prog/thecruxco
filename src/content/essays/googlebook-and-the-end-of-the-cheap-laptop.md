The Chromebook's whole pitch was that it was cheap. Google's new laptop leads with the opposite.

## What happened

On September 15 in New York, Google previewed the Googlebook — a new premium laptop category built on Android rather than ChromeOS, made with Acer, ASUS, Dell, HP and Lenovo. It comes with a lit "Glowbar" strip on the lid, a "Magic Pointer" AI cursor, the Play Store, the full Chrome browser, and Gemini wired into the system layer. Reviewers peg the price at or above the $499–$999 band of today's Chromebook Plus models; pre-orders are reported to open September 21. Google framed the shift plainly: "Over 15 years ago, we introduced the Chromebook, a laptop built for a cloud-first world. Now, as we are moving from an operating system to an intelligence system, we see an opportunity to rethink laptops again."

## Why it matters

The Chromebook was one of the most quietly consequential products of the last decade. It stripped the laptop down to a browser, sold for as little as a couple hundred dollars, and put a generation of students online — in American school districts, and across price-sensitive markets like India, where "cheap and it just works" was the entire value proposition. The Googlebook points the other way: premium materials, higher prices, AI as the headline feature. Google insists this isn't a funeral — it says ChromeOS continues and existing Chromebooks keep their promised updates. But money and attention are a kind of vote, and this vote is going upmarket.

## The crux

Strip away the Glowbar and the Magic Pointer and the real move is a change of identity. For 15 years, Google's answer to "what is a laptop" was: as little as possible, in the cloud, for cheap. The Googlebook's answer is: as much as possible, powered by Gemini, for a premium. Sit with the phrase Google chose — "moving from an operating system to an intelligence system." It sounds like marketing, and partly it is. But it also describes the logic gripping the whole industry: AI is expensive to run, and expensive to run means someone has to pay for it. A cheap, plain, reliable laptop doesn't have an obvious seat in an "intelligence system" priced to cover the cost of the intelligence.

## What to take away

- If you buy or manage budget laptops — for a school, a family, a first job — don't panic, but don't assume the cheap tier stays a priority. Google says Chromebooks live on; watch where the new models, the marketing, and the best chips actually go over the next year. Direction of travel beats press releases.
- When any company reframes a product as "AI-first," translate it: the feature is sometimes real, and the price is always real. Ask what you're getting that you couldn't before — and whether you were happier with the version that just opened a browser and cost $250.

Google made the laptop cheap enough for everyone. Now it's betting its future on making it expensive again.
